The Deccan

Hyderabadi Cuisine

Persian court cooking meets Telugu spice tradition — four centuries in the making, one dish at the center of it.

Two dynasties, one fusion cuisine

Hyderabadi cuisine is a fusion in the most literal sense, and its history is unusually well documented because it's a court cuisine — food shaped deliberately by ruling dynasties rather than evolving purely from agriculture and geography. The Qutb Shahi dynasty, which ruled the Golconda Sultanate from the 16th to 17th century, brought Persian court cooking traditions to the Deccan. When the Mughal Empire later absorbed the region and the Nizams (the Asaf Jahi dynasty) took power in the 18th century, their kitchens fused that Persian-Mughal foundation with the spice-forward Telugu and Marathwada cooking already established locally. The result is a cuisine that is simultaneously rich and fragrant (from its Persian-Mughal half) and sharply spiced (from its Deccan half).

The dum technique

What actually distinguishes Hyderabadi biryani technically from other Indian biryanis is the cooking method, not just the spice blend. In the "kachi" (raw) style, marinated raw meat is layered directly with partially cooked rice, and the sealed pot is then slow-cooked over low heat (dum) so the rice finishes cooking in the steam and juices released by the meat, rather than the two being cooked separately and combined afterward. This layering-and-sealing technique — often literally sealing the pot lid with dough to trap steam — is a Persian court-kitchen method, and its survival in Hyderabad is a direct culinary trace of the Nizams' Persian-influenced kitchens.

Hyderabadi biryani isn't just a recipe — the layering-and-sealing technique itself is a 300-year-old Persian court method, still practiced exactly as it was.

Haleem and the Nizami table

Haleem — a dense, slow-pounded stew of wheat, lentils, and meat — has similarly deep roots, tracing back to Arab and Persian meat-and-wheat porridge traditions (related to the Middle Eastern harees) brought into the Nizams' kitchens and adapted with local spice. It's now strongly associated with Ramadan in Hyderabad, served as an iftar dish across the city each evening during the fasting month.

Quick facts

Region
Deccan Plateau (Hyderabad, Telangana)
Staple grain
Rice
Defining technique
Dum — sealed-pot, layered slow-cooking
Key historical influence
Qutb Shahi & Nizami (Asaf Jahi) court kitchens, 16th–20th century
Signature dish
Hyderabadi biryani

Signature dishes

  • Hyderabadi biryani — layered, dum-cooked rice and marinated meat.
  • Haleem — a slow-pounded wheat, lentil, and meat stew, a Ramadan specialty.
  • Mirchi ka salan — a tangy chili curry in a peanut-sesame gravy.
  • Double ka meetha — a bread pudding fried and soaked in sweetened milk, of Persian dessert lineage.
  • Qubani ka meetha — a stewed apricot dessert, served with cream.
