Why regional

One country, dozens of kitchens

"Khaana" simply means food in Hindi and Urdu, and it stretches across every regional kitchen on this site. What lands on a plate in Amritsar shares almost nothing with what's served in Kochi, and a kitchen in Kolkata runs on its own logic, as does one in Ahmedabad. Climate, soil, trade routes, empires, and religion each left a mark on a different part of the subcontinent. The result is a patchwork of distinct traditions, each with its own staple grain, cooking fat and history.

Each page follows one region's food back to its roots, the geography that shaped it, the history that flavored it, and the dishes it is known for today.

Sindhi

A cuisine that crossed a border in 1947 and kept going. Sai bhaji, koki and Sunday dal pakwan.

Parsi

Persian technique on Gujarati ingredients: sweet, sour and hot at once, with an egg on almost everything.

Anglo-Indian

The food of the railway colonies. Mulligatawny, country captain and cutlets, British method with Indian spicing.
Community

Indo-Chinese

Invented in Kolkata's Tangra and a Bombay cricket club. Soy and vinegar meeting green chilli and garlic.
North

Awadhi/Lucknowi

The Nawabs of Oudh's refined court cuisine: dum cooking, melt-in-the-mouth kebabs, and understated spicing.
Himalaya

Pahari

Hill cooking from Himachal and Uttarakhand: yogurt gravies, dried pulses and grains that survive a winter.
North

Kashmiri

Persian-influenced Himalayan cooking built around the wazwan feast and a slow-cooked, saffron-scented restraint.
North

Punjabi

Tandoors, dairy, and wheat. The food of the fertile plains and the diaspora's most familiar Indian flavors.
West

Rajasthani

Desert cooking. Long shelf life, minimal water, and dairy standing in for fresh vegetables.
West

Gujarati

India's great vegetarian cuisine. A deliberate balance of sweet, salty, and sour on a single thali.
West

Maharashtrian

A coastal Konkan tradition and a spicy Deccan plateau tradition, sharing one state and one street-food capital.
Coastal

Goan

450 years of Portuguese rule gave India vinegar, chilies, and pork vindaloo, cooked the Konkani way.
South

Karnataka

Three cuisines in one state: Udupi temple kitchens, the ragi-and-jowar interior, and the seafood coast.
South

Kerala

The Malabar spice coast: coconut over ghee, a Jewish-Christian-Muslim-Hindu culinary mix, and a 26-dish vegetarian feast.
South

Tamil Nadu

Rice, lentils and fermentation. A 2,000-year-old vegetarian tradition built around the dosa, the thali and the Chettinad kitchen.
South

Andhra

India's hottest cooking. Guntur chilli, sour gongura, and a rice-and-pappu plate built around podi powders.
Deccan

Hyderabadi

Where Mughal court cuisine met Telugu spice traditions under the Nizams and biryani became an art form.
East

Odia

Temple food and coastal rice cooking: pakhala, dalma, and the giant kitchen at the Jagannath Temple in Puri.
East

Bihari

Sattu as everyday protein, litti baked in embers, and sweets that each belong to a named town.
East

Bengali

Mustard oil, river fish, and a five-course structure that treats sweetness as a savory tool rather than a dessert.
Northeast

Northeast Indian

Eight states, dozens of tribal cuisines, and a shared logic of fermentation, bamboo, and minimal spice.
