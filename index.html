Why regional

One country, a dozen kitchens

"Khaana" simply means food in Hindi and Urdu, but there is no single Indian cuisine. What lands on a plate in Amritsar shares almost nothing with what's served in Kochi, and a kitchen in Kolkata runs on entirely different logic than one in Ahmedabad. Climate, soil, trade routes, empires, and religion each left their mark on a different part of the subcontinent, and the result is a patchwork of genuinely distinct and delicious culinary traditions rather than one cuisine with regional variations.

Each page here traces one region's food back to its roots including the geography that shaped it, the historical influences that flavored it, and the dishes that define it today.

Eight regional cuisines

North

Punjabi

Tandoors, dairy, and wheat. The food of the fertile plains and the diaspora's most familiar Indian flavors.
South

South Indian

Rice, lentils, and fermentation. A 2,000-year-old vegetarian tradition built around the dosa and the thali.
East

Bengali

Mustard oil, river fish, and a five-course structure that treats sweetness as a savory tool, not just dessert.
West

Gujarati

India's great vegetarian cuisine. A deliberate balance of sweet, salty, and sour on a single thali.
West

Rajasthani

Desert cooking. Long shelf life, minimal water, and dairy standing in for fresh vegetables.
West

Maharashtrian

A coastal Konkan tradition and a spicy Deccan plateau tradition, sharing one state and one street-food capital.
Deccan

Hyderabadi

Where Mughal court cuisine met Telugu spice traditions under the Nizams and biryani became an art form.
Coastal

Goan

450 years of Portuguese rule gave India vinegar, chilies, and pork vindaloo, cooked the Konkani way.
