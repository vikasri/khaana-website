Awadhi / Lucknowi Cuisine

The refined court cooking of the Nawabs of Oudh: sealed-pot slow cooking, whisper-quiet spicing, and kebabs engineered to dissolve on the tongue.

A cuisine built for a court, not a marketplace

Awadhi cuisine takes its name from Awadh (Oudh), the region around Lucknow ruled by the Nawabs of Oudh from the early 18th century until the British annexed it in 1856. The Nawabs began as Mughal provincial governors and, as central Mughal authority weakened, became independent rulers in practice while keeping Mughal court culture, including its cooking, as their model. What developed in Lucknow's royal kitchens over the following century was less about heat or heavy spicing and more about refinement: technique and aroma over raw chili, and a level of culinary craftsmanship aimed at an audience of one, the Nawab, rather than a marketplace of customers.

Dum pukht and a famine-relief legend

Dum pukht, literally "breath-cooking," is Awadhi cuisine's defining technique: meat and rice sealed into a heavy pot, often with a rim of dough, and cooked slowly over a low, steady fire so the dish finishes in its own trapped steam and aroma rather than direct heat. Lucknow legend ties its popularization to a famine around 1784, when Nawab Asaf-ud-Daula commissioned the Bara Imambara as a relief-work project; food for the laborers was reportedly cooked overnight in huge sealed deghs (cauldrons) so it would be ready whenever a shift ended. Whether or not that origin story is literally true, it captures what the technique was built for: cooking that holds its quality over long, unpredictable stretches of time.

"Dum pukht" literally means "breath-cooking": food finished in its own trapped steam inside a sealed pot, not over open heat.

Galouti kebab: built for a Nawab who had lost his teeth

Lucknow's best-known dish has a similarly specific legend behind it. The galouti kebab ("melt-in-the-mouth" kebab) is said to have been created for an aging Nawab, often identified as Asaf-ud-Daula or Wajid Ali Shah, who loved kebabs but had lost his teeth. The solution was to pound the meat exceptionally fine and work in a complex spice blend, popularly (if probably apocryphally) said to run to over a hundred spices, to produce a kebab soft enough to require no chewing at all. Lucknow's Tunday Kebabi, named for its founder's tunda (disabled arm), still trades on a version of that closely guarded recipe today.

Biryani, the Awadhi way

Awadhi biryani is usually cooked pukki style: rice and meat are parboiled and cooked separately, then layered and finished together under dum, giving a drier, more distinctly layered result than Hyderabad's kacchi biryani, where raw marinated meat and raw rice go into the pot together from the start. The two techniques, developed in two different Nawabi courts, remain the clearest fault line in Indian biryani-making. Awadhi cooking's reach also extended well beyond Lucknow: when the British exiled the last Nawab, Wajid Ali Shah, to Calcutta in 1856, he brought his court cooks with him, and Bengal's own Kolkata-style biryani, notably distinguished by the addition of potato, is usually traced to that exiled Awadhi kitchen adapting to leaner circumstances.

Quick facts

Region
Awadh, central-northern Uttar Pradesh (capital: Lucknow)
Staple grain
Wheat and rice
Defining technique
Dum (sealed slow-cooking) and finely pounded meat
Key historical moment
Semi-independent rule by the Nawabs of Oudh, 18th century to 1856
Signature dish
Galouti kebab

Signature dishes

  • Galouti kebab: finely pounded, heavily spiced kebab, said to have been created for a toothless Nawab.
  • Lucknowi (pukki) dum biryani: rice and meat parboiled separately, layered, then finished under dum.
  • Nihari: a slow-simmered meat stew, traditionally a breakfast dish.
  • Sheermal: a mildly sweet, saffron-tinted flatbread.
  • Zarda: sweet saffron rice, often served to close a meal.
